01 — Overview

Designed for the cook who
already knows how to cook.

A self-directed iOS concept — no PM, no engineering, no stakeholders. Two surfaces designed from the system up: Recipe Detail (where trust is established) and Cook Mode (where the recipe is actually used). Mid-fidelity is intentional — evaluate the decisions, not the visual finish.

What it is
Curated search for authentic Asian recipes
Only recipes from culturally credible, vetted sources surface in results. Korean Bapsang shows up. Allrecipes doesn't. The editorial layer does the trust work the algorithm can't.
Who it's for
Cooks who already cook
Not beginners. Not infinite-recipe-browsers. Someone competent in the kitchen who keeps getting disappointing results when cooking dishes outside their own culinary background — and suspects the recipe was the problem.
Scope this phase
Find → Read → Cook
Three flows, each designed for its context. Search returns only vetted sources. Recipe view shows attribution and handles substitutions. Cook Mode is a separate surface for the actual kitchen.
Process
System → mid-fi → Figma hi-fi
Design system and brand board built before a single screen. This mid-fidelity prototype validates structure and decisions. Hi-fi execution in Figma is next.
System Architecture

Full scope of Find → Read → Cook, mapped before any screens were designed. The deferred auth gate — sign-in only when a user tries to save — is an intentional architectural decision, not an afterthought.

02 — The Problem

The gap isn't search.
It's trust.

You can find a Kimchi Jjigae recipe in seconds. Maangchi has millions of fans. Cultural authorship is visible. And yet a competent cook still can't tell — before spending money on ingredients — whether this particular recipe will actually taste right in their kitchen. That's the gap. Trust isn't a follower count.

How it works today

How it should work

Find it. Know you can trust it.

  • ◆

What the research found

Three signals — all organic, no user interviews. Secondary research from forums, cooking blogs, and a Reddit thread that sat unanswered for a year.

r/KoreanFood · Secondary research · H1 + H2 + H3

"I've tried Maangchi's recipe, Seonkyoung Longest's, and Aaron & Claire on YouTube. My wife thinks they're just ok. Maybe it's me."

He had the popularity signals. He had the cultural authorship. He still couldn't get it right — and blamed himself. One reply, unhelpful. Thread unanswered for a year. This is the whole product rationale in one Reddit post: trust isn't a follower count or a cultural credential. It's editorial judgment applied to sources. That's what the app provides.

Sunset Magazine + Madeleine Kitchen · Secondary research · H2

"Many sites are run by people posting random ideas, hoping to land on page one of a search engine. Throwing keywords into a recipe doesn't guarantee flavour or accuracy."

SEO incentives and user-generated volume have broken recipe search by design. The information architecture of the web actively surfaces the worst results first. This isn't fixable with a better algorithm — it requires a curated layer above search.

Discuss Cooking forum + Plan to Eat blog · Secondary research · H3

"I get tired of sites that parrot what the other cooking blogs say. I'm looking for a bit more authenticity. Any ideas?"

The workaround already exists: experienced cooks build personal stashes of trusted sources over years. What Umami does is give everyone that trust layer from day one — without requiring years of accumulated kitchen judgment to know which sites are worth bookmarking.

04 — Recipe Detail

The recipe as an
editorial object.

Recipe Detail is the core surface — where a cook decides whether to commit. It has to earn trust immediately and get out of the way just as fast. Every element is there because it earns its place.

Direction Exploration

The core layout question: where does trust live? Early explorations tested trust signals buried in body text vs. a dedicated trust block vs. attribution above the fold. The handwritten annotation made the decision clear: "attribution = trust, not followers."

Hero, attribution, and a translucent nav that earns its place.

The first screen a cook sees must answer two questions instantly: "Is this the right dish?" and "Should I trust this recipe?" The hero image handles the first. Attribution handles the second.

1
Translucent nav, transparent over hero. The nav bar is invisible over the full-bleed hero image. On scroll, it collapses to a solid translucent bar with the recipe title — following iOS presentation grammar exactly, so the UI feels native from first touch.
2
Honest attribution, not aggregation. "Adapted from Hyosun, Korean Bapsang" is displayed prominently — not buried in a footnote. If a cook trusts Korean Bapsang, this is the signal they need. If they don't know the source, the attribution gives them somewhere to look.
3
Time, yield, and a single CTA above the fold. 15 min active / 35 min total, serves 4, and one clear action: Cook Mode. A cook shouldn't have to scroll to find out whether this recipe is feasible for tonight.

Ingredients that respond to how you cook.

Two controls at the top of ingredients that should exist in every recipe app — and mostly don't. Scaling and unit conversion are utility features, but they change whether someone can actually use the recipe.

1
Serves stepper with live rescaling. Tap + or − and every ingredient quantity updates instantly. Cooking for 2 instead of 4 means half the pork belly, half the kimchi — not mental arithmetic mid-prep.
2
US / Metric toggle. A persistent toggle at the top of the ingredients section. The preference is remembered. No hunting through settings.
3
AI ingredient substitution. Swappable ingredients show an indicator. Tapping opens a swap panel — options ranked by taste impact, with a note on how each swap changes the dish. Reverting restores the original. The authenticity thesis made into a feature.

Substitutions framed by taste impact, not just availability.

Most substitution features answer "what can I use instead?" Umami answers a harder question: "if I make this swap, how much does it move the dish from authentic?" That distinction is the product thesis made tangible.

1
Bottom sheet presentation. Follows iOS presentation grammar for focused, dismissible tasks. The sheet appears over the recipe without replacing it — the cook can see context while making a swap decision.
2
Taste impact scale. Each substitute is ranked 1–3 dots: how much does this swap move the dish from authentic? A cook using miso paste instead of doenjang deserves to know that's a meaningful change, not just a convenient one.
3
Revert to original. Any swap can be undone. The original ingredient is always restorable — no destructive state changes.

Key Cook Mode Decisions
Decision 01
Modal, not a pushed page

Cook Mode is a full-screen modal cover — not a navigation push. It gets a single ✕ close affordance, not a back chevron. This isn't aesthetic preference; it's iOS presentation grammar. Cook Mode is a distinct mode of use. The modal model communicates that clearly: entering Cook Mode is a commitment, and leaving it requires a deliberate action.

→ Full-screen cover (UIModalPresentationFullScreen). ✕ to dismiss — not a back chevron.
Decision 02
Confirm-on-exit with real resume

Closing Cook Mode past step 1 triggers an iOS action sheet: Save & Exit (keeps your place in state) or Discard Progress (destructive, shown in red per iOS convention). Returning to the recipe later offers Resume at Step N or Cook Again. This isn't cosmetic — progress is real state, persisted, and restorable. Interruptions are part of cooking.

→ Action sheet (UIAlertControllerStyleActionSheet). Destructive action in red, secondary outlined.
Decision 03
Dark surface for kitchen ergonomics

Cook Mode uses a dark surface — not because dark mode is fashionable, but because kitchens are bright. A white screen at arm's length, glanced between stirring, creates glare and makes text hard to read. A dark, high-contrast layout is readable from across a counter with messy hands. Large type. High contrast. The visual shift also signals the mode change — entering Cook Mode feels different from browsing.

→ bg: #1c1108 (warm near-black). Type: var(--cream) at 28–36pt for step text.
Decision 04
Completion as a moment, not a prompt

Finishing the last step yields a dish complete state — not an immediate rating modal. The cook just made something. The app acknowledges it. Then it surfaces Save + 5-star rating + Share — actions that make sense after the accomplishment, not as an interruption of it. The completion moment is also the natural, unforced moment to ask for a rating.

→ Completion state → rating + save CTA. No forced review prompt mid-session.
06 — Platform Decisions

iOS isn't a skin.
It's a contract.

Every platform has conventions users have internalized without knowing it. Breaking them creates friction that designers blame on the content, when it's actually the container. These are the decisions that keep Umami feeling native.

Applied Decisions
Correct presentation grammar
Bottom sheets for focused tasks (ingredient swaps, sign-in). Full-screen modal cover for Cook Mode — not a pushed page. Action sheets for destructive confirms. Each presentation style is semantically correct for its use case, not chosen for aesthetics.
UISheet · UIModalPresentationFullScreen · UIAlertControllerStyleActionSheet
Translucent nav with scroll collapse
Nav bar is transparent over the full-bleed recipe hero image. On scroll, it collapses to solid translucent with the recipe title — the iOS large-title pattern that users have internalized without ever naming it. Violating it would feel wrong without anyone knowing why.
UINavigationBar · transparent → blur on scroll
Destructive actions in red
"Discard Progress" in the stop-cooking action sheet uses red — iOS convention for irreversible actions. A cook who just spent 20 minutes on step 4 needs that visual signal to register before tapping. Not decoration. A safety mechanism.
.destructive role · UIAlertAction
Screen stay-awake during active cooking
Cook Mode disables the idle timer while a step timer is running. The screen stays on. A cook shouldn't have to unlock their phone mid-simmer with floury hands to see what comes next — the app knows it's being actively used even when the user isn't touching the screen.

07 — What's Next

Mid-fidelity proves
the decisions. Figma makes them real.

The interactive prototype exists to validate structure and interaction decisions before committing to high-fidelity execution. That validation is done. The next phase is craft.

01
Hi-fi execution in Figma. The design system is built. Decisions are locked. Next step is applying the full Craft Cookbook visual language — Brick × Wheat palette, texture, illustration — to Recipe Detail and Cook Mode, then expanding to search and onboarding.
02
Validate the trust signals before committing to hi-fi. Attribution above the fold and deferred auth are architectural bets — not yet confirmed by user research. The key question: does attribution read as a trust signal before a cook decides to scroll? Does the curated-source model earn enough confidence on its own, or does it need explicit explanation? Those answers change the visual hierarchy of Recipe Detail, and they should be answered before the hi-fi pass begins.
03
Motion and handoff spec. Cook Mode modal entry, timer-fired state, and dish completion all have implied motion that needs to be specified before handoff. Motion that communicates state, not decoration. Component annotations and token mapping follow — so a developer can build from the file without a walkthrough call.
